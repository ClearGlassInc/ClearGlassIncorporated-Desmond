AI Agents Are the New Insider Threat — and Your Security Program Can't See Them

Insider threat programs watch people: badge logs, DLP alerts, sentiment flags. Meanwhile the fastest-growing population of insiders in your company doesn't have a badge, a manager, or a bad day. It has an API key.

D
Desmond Otieno Odhiambo
Founder & Software Architect, ClearGlass Inc. · July 2, 2026 · 9 min read

TL;DR

  • AI agents are insiders by definition: they hold credentials, act inside the perimeter, and are trusted by default — the exact profile insider-threat programs were built to watch.
  • They break every human-shaped control. No badge, no manager, no behavioral baseline. UEBA tuned on people goes blind on agents that legitimately work at 3 a.m. at machine speed.
  • The failure mode isn't malice — it's obedience. An over-permissioned agent doing exactly what it was told, at scale, is indistinguishable from an attack by the time you notice.
  • AIRF is the response: four controls — Identity, Intent, Blast Radius, Ledger — that turn an unaccountable insider into a governed one.

Every insider-threat program in the world is built on a quiet assumption: insiders are people. They have identities in the HR system, managers who notice mood changes, working hours, and career incentives. The entire discipline — from UEBA baselines to exit-interview offboarding — is a behavioral science of employees.

That assumption just expired. The average enterprise is now deploying software that holds standing credentials, operates inside the trust boundary, takes actions on production systems, and was granted its access by someone who no longer remembers doing it. That is the textbook definition of an insider. We just don't call it one, because it doesn't eat lunch in the cafeteria.

Worse: the agent population is growing faster than the human one, its permissions are broader on day one than most employees earn in five years, and it never gets an exit interview.

"Your next insider incident won't be a disgruntled employee. It will be an over-permissioned agent doing exactly what it was told."

01Why agents break every human-shaped control

Take the standard insider-threat toolkit and run it against an AI agent:

  • Behavioral baselines (UEBA). Built to flag a human who suddenly downloads 10,000 files at 3 a.m. An agent does that legitimately, every night. Machine-speed, off-hours, high-volume activity is its normal — so the baseline either alerts on everything or learns to ignore the one actor most capable of mass exfiltration.
  • Identity governance. Joiner-mover-leaver processes assume an HR record. Agents are spun up by an engineer with a service account and a Slack message. There is no mover process when the agent's task changes, and no leaver process when the project dies — the credentials just keep working.
  • Least privilege. Humans accumulate permissions over years. Agents get theirs in one YAML file, usually copied from the last agent, usually broad "so it doesn't break." Nobody re-reviews it because nobody owns it.
  • Deterrence. Policy, training, and consequences shape human behavior. An agent cannot be deterred, only constrained. If the constraint isn't in the architecture, it doesn't exist.

The uncomfortable summary: the controls are aimed at motive, and agents don't have one. They have instructions — from an operator, a prompt, a poisoned document, or a compromised upstream tool. Intent lives outside the actor. Nothing in the classic insider playbook was designed for that.

02The failure mode is obedience, not betrayal

Human insider incidents are rare because betrayal is rare. Agent incidents will be common because obedience at scale is the product. The three patterns we model for clients:

  1. The faithful executor. The agent was told to "clean up stale records" and its definition of stale was wrong. It deleted 40,000 rows correctly, quickly, and with valid credentials. No alert fired because nothing was anomalous — for the agent.
  2. The confused deputy. An attacker can't touch the CRM, but the customer-service agent can. So the attacker doesn't attack the CRM; they attack the agent's inputs — a crafted email, a poisoned knowledge-base article — and let the agent's legitimate permissions do the work. The agent becomes the insider on someone else's behalf.
  3. The orphan. The pilot ended, the team moved on, the agent kept its tokens. Eighteen months later it's an unowned, unmonitored, fully-credentialed actor that nobody would recognize as missing — because nobody remembers it exists.
Field note

In every environment we've assessed, the question "how many agents and service identities can write to production, and who owns each one?" has produced the same answer: a number nobody knew, and a list nobody owned. Start there. The gap between those two answers is your insider-threat surface.

03AIRF: the Agent Insider Risk Framework

The response isn't a new detection signature. It's a governance architecture. AIRF is the model we implement at ClearGlass — four controls, in order, each one meaningless without the one before it:

AIRF / 01
Identity
Every agent is a first-class identity: owner, purpose, expiry. No shared keys, no orphans.
AIRF / 02
Intent
Actions are checked against declared purpose — not just permissions — before execution.
AIRF / 03
Blast Radius
Cap what one agent can destroy in one hour: rate limits, scopes, spend ceilings, kill switch.
AIRF / 04
Ledger
Append-only record of every material action, with the instruction that caused it.

1. Identity — make the agent a who, not a what

Every agent gets a unique, non-shared identity with three mandatory fields: a named human owner, a declared purpose in one sentence, and an expiry date. If any field is missing, the credentials don't issue. Expiry is the quiet killer feature: orphans stop being possible when access dies by default and renewal requires the owner to say "yes, this still exists for a reason."

2. Intent — verify the why, not just the may

Permissions answer "may this identity do this?" Intent verification answers the question that actually matters for an insider: "does this action serve this agent's declared purpose?" A customer-service agent may have CRM write access, but a request to export the full contact table doesn't match "resolve individual customer tickets" — and gets routed to a human regardless of what the token allows. This is the control that catches the confused deputy, because the attacker can hijack the agent's permissions but not its declared purpose.

3. Blast Radius — assume compromise, cap the damage

Design every agent as if it will one day act maliciously — through hijack, bug, or bad instruction — and ask one question: what is the maximum damage it can do in one hour before a human notices? Then make that number survivable:

READ
Observation tierQuery, summarize, draft. Rate-limited, but freely granted. Compromise here is an information problem, not an integrity one.
WRITE
Bounded-change tierScoped writes with per-hour caps, per-record limits, and automatic rollback windows. One agent, one domain — never "the database."
MOVE
Irreversible tierMoney, deletion, external send, credential changes. Human approval required, hard spend/volume ceilings, and a kill switch that one on-call engineer can pull without a change ticket.

4. Ledger — the exit interview an agent can't refuse

Every material action lands in an append-only ledger: what was done, under whose identity, and — critically — which instruction caused it. When something goes wrong, the ledger answers in minutes what incident response would otherwise take weeks to reconstruct: was this a bug, a hijacked input, or an operator's bad instruction? Without the causal chain from instruction to action, every agent incident is unattributable — and unattributable insiders never stop being a problem.

"Permissions tell you what an agent may do. Only intent and a ledger tell you whether it should have — and why it did."

04Where to start on Monday

  1. Census. Enumerate every non-human identity that can write to production — agents, service accounts, bot tokens. The number will surprise you.
  2. Ownership. Assign a named human owner to each. Anything unclaimed in 30 days gets its credentials revoked. (The things that break were your orphans.)
  3. Expiry. Convert standing credentials to expiring ones. Default 90 days. Renewal is one click for the owner and impossible for anyone else.
  4. Tier the blast radius. Classify each agent READ / WRITE / MOVE. Everything in MOVE gets a human approval gate and a kill switch this quarter.
  5. Turn on the ledger. Append-only, includes the triggering instruction. Even a basic version converts your next agent incident from a mystery into a query.

None of this slows the roadmap. It's the opposite: the companies deploying agents fastest in regulated industries are the ones that can show an auditor exactly who their agents are, what they're for, and what they did last Tuesday. Governance isn't the tax on agentic AI. It's the license to run it.
