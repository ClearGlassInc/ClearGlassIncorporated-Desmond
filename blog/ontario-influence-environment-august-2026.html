Assessment: separate the categories before drawing conclusions

Analytical standard

“Psychological operations” is a military term and should not be used as a catch-all label for political advertising, government communications, closed media events or online misinformation. The measurable issue is influence: who is communicating, who pays, what is disclosed, what evidence supports the message, and whether scrutiny is restricted.

The highest-confidence domestic finding is financial and documentary: Ontario recorded $111.9 million in government advertising in fiscal 2024-25, then continued substantial “Protect Ontario” advertising into 2025-26. The strongest foreign-interference finding is institutional: Canada’s Foreign Influence Transparency and Accountability Act regime came into force on August 4, 2026, while CSIS continues to identify multiple foreign states as active interference and espionage threats.

1. Domestic state messaging: what the evidence supports

Record-scale public advertising

Public reporting based on Ontario Auditor General findings places 2024-25 government advertising at $111.9 million, $8.4 million above the prior year. The Auditor General’s criticism was not that all government advertising is illegitimate; it was that specific campaigns appeared designed to improve public impressions of Ontario’s condition without providing corresponding service or program information.

Protect Ontario as a government and partisan-coded phrase

“Protect Ontario” appears in current Ontario government advertising and also in Ontario PC Party political branding. That overlap is an objective transparency issue because identical or near-identical slogans can make the boundary between publicly funded information and partisan persuasion harder for audiences to distinguish. Overlap alone does not establish an unlawful campaign; intent, placement, content, timing and election-law treatment matter.

What can be measured

The defensible OSINT approach is to collect media-buy records, creative variants, dates, platforms, geographic targeting, ministry ownership and election-period timing. Those variables can be audited. Claims about motive should be separated from documented spend and distribution data.

2. Foreign information and influence: a different threat class

Canada’s new Foreign Influence Transparency framework entered into force on August 4, 2026. The commissioner’s office administers a public registry for arrangements involving foreign principals when statutory conditions are met. Current federal guidance states that new registrable arrangements generally must be filed within 14 days, while qualifying arrangements that existed before August 4 have until October 3, 2026.

CSIS’s 2025 public reporting identified the People’s Republic of China, India, Russia, Iran and Pakistan as the main perpetrators of foreign interference and espionage against Canada during that reporting year. This is materially different from domestic government advertising: foreign interference is defined around clandestine, deceptive, coercive or threatening activity detrimental to Canadian interests.

Elections Canada has also documented AI-generated videos containing inaccurate election information and described the broader evolution of disinformation tactics. That evidence supports monitoring synthetic-media and coordinated-amplification risks without assuming that every false or polarizing post is state-directed.

4. Transparency and accountability gaps

  • Aggregate spending hides targeting. Province-wide totals do not reveal riding-level frequency, demographic targeting or platform-specific placement.
  • Slogan overlap creates ambiguity. When government and party communications use the same branding language, disclosure and context become more important.
  • Registry effectiveness is not yet measurable. The federal foreign-influence registry is new; compliance volumes, enforcement patterns and public utility will need time-series analysis.
  • Attribution remains the hard problem. High-volume misinformation is not automatically a foreign operation. Account provenance, coordination signals, infrastructure and authoritative attribution matter.
  • Absence of evidence is not evidence of an operation. This brief does not treat Canadian Armed Forces PSYOPS doctrine or training as proof of domestic influence activity directed at Ontarians.

5. Monitoring KPIs

Track

Advertising spend

Total cost, ministry, production vendor, placement vendor, medium and geographic targeting.

Track

Media access

Open vs. closed events, invitation rules, livestream availability, question periods and independent footage.

Track

Election adjacency

Ad dates and locations relative to writ periods, by-election ridings and Elections Ontario guidance or rulings.

Track

Foreign registry

Number of filings, foreign principals, activity types, compliance notices and enforcement actions.

The objective is not to label every persuasive communication as hostile. It is to make influence visible enough that the public can inspect funding, provenance, targeting, disclosure and accountability.
